01 / THE PLACE

A harmless name for a lethal program

The official title sounded like public health: the Epidemic Prevention and Water Supply Department. In practice, the Imperial Japanese Army’s Unit 731 developed biological weapons and conducted fatal experiments on people held in its custody. Its main complex was at Pingfang, near Harbin in occupied northeastern China.1, 2, 3

Physician and military officer Shiro Ishii became the central figure in this program. Chinese prisoners were among its principal experimental subjects, alongside Russians and others. Thousands died in experiments during the program’s wartime operation. These were captives, not volunteers.2

The medical language can hide the purpose. Researchers wanted to understand how to cause disease, how it progressed in human bodies, and how to make it useful in war.2

02 / THE EXPERIMENTS

What they did to people

The surviving record includes deliberate infection, exposure to biological weapons, and experiments on injuries.

Deliberate infection

Researchers infected captives with diseases including plague, anthrax, cholera and typhoid, then observed the results. These experiments served a military biological-warfare program.2

Vivisection

Historical accounts describe operations on living prisoners, including procedures without anesthesia, to examine organs and disease damage before death. “Vivisection” is the term for this kind of cutting into a living subject.3

Frostbite and cold injury

Prisoners were deliberately subjected to freezing injuries so researchers could study tissue damage and compare methods of warming affected limbs.3

Weapon trials and attacks

The research included trials of biological weapons on captives. Japanese biological warfare also extended beyond the laboratory to attacks on Chinese populations.1, 2

03 / THE BARGAIN

Information took priority over prosecution

After Japan’s defeat, American investigators sought the program’s findings. U.S. authorities protected Ishii and other participants from prosecution in exchange for cooperation.1, 2

“documentary immunity from war crimes will be provided to Ishii and others in exchange for cooperation and full disclosure”
U.S. National Archives documentary guide, quoting a 1947 SCAP G-2 record, JWC 152.1

The same guide identifies lengthy reports on anthrax, glanders and plague that drew on Japanese human experiments, postwar interviews and work by U.S. Army biological-warfare laboratories at Fort Detrick.1

Not every participant escaped proceedings: the Soviet Union prosecuted twelve members of the Japanese biological-warfare program at Khabarovsk in 1949. There was no comparable American-led prosecution of Unit 731’s physicians.2
